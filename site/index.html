The Proof Economy

Prove a circuit once, in public, and let every system built on it inherit the proof.

A chip is a hierarchy of blocks, and before any of it is sent to a foundry each block must be shown correct: a mask set at a modern node costs one to two million dollars, and industry surveys put first-silicon success at 14 percent in 2024 and 5 percent in 2026. Today that verification is repeated. Every company that uses an adder or a floating-point unit re-verifies its own copy in private, and the work is thrown away when the project ends. A proof of a block does not depend on who uses the block. If the proof is public and content-addressed, the next team inherits it instead of repeating it, and a larger system is proved by reference to the proofs of its parts without ever opening them. This site is the public instance of that idea: how a proof is graded, what a certificate is worth, where it settles, and a free service that tells cells apart for a given net.

Two words, then the ladder

A block is any unit of the hierarchy with an interface of its own: a cell of a few transistors, a component such as an adder or a decoder, an assembly such as a multiplier, a system such as an accelerator. A certificate is the public record of a block's proof: the content hash of its design, the content hash of its interface, the level at which it was proved, and the content hash of the proof of record. A certificate for a composed block also names the certificates of its children. Anyone can check that the proof exists and matches; nobody needs the design to do so.

One instrument, two halves: correctness and cost

A certificate binds two facts about a block, and the economy needs both. The first is correctness: the block computes the right function, proved once, so a larger design inherits it without re-checking. The proof ladder below settles this, and the mathematics proves it. The second is cost: what the block spends in the three physical resources a circuit cannot escape, area, time, and energy, with information as the yardstick that makes them comparable (Shannon's measure of information, Landauer's floor of kT ln2 of energy per bit a calculation destroys, and the area-time bound of Thompson and of Mead and Conway). The engineering measures this on one open bench. Correctness is why a system may inherit a block; cost is why a designer chooses one block over another that computes the same function. There is rarely a single best block, because a design binds on whichever resource it is short of, so a certificate publishes a point on the cost surface rather than a ranking. The certificate carries both halves, and the Arbitrum Stylus contracts settle it as one asset. The cell-differentiation service lower on this page is the cost half made public for one family of cells; the ladder is the correctness half made public for every block.

How a proof is graded: the levels of proof

Every certificate names the level at which its block is proved. The ladder runs from the strongest bounded proof to the one that scales without bound. Exhaustive checking costs 2 to the power of the input width, so it stops at small blocks; composition is the top and it is closed: a composed block is a part for the next composition.

What a certificate is worth: two licenses

Every certificate carries two licenses. The protocol, meaning the contracts that settle them, takes nothing from either.

Reference: the commons

Cost recovery, never rent

The reference license is the right to name a certificate as a child of a larger proof. Foundry PDK primitives are free to reference forever. Every other certificate recovers only its listing gas, the on-chain fee its lister paid to list it, from the certificates one level up that use it directly; the recursion beneath is never charged. Collection stops at that gas, then the certificate is free. Whoever lists a better implementation of the same interface pays the remaining listing cost of the block it beats: beat or fork.

Manufacturing: the designer's product

$100 per mask set, SoC down

The manufacturing license is the right to put a design into a fabricated system on chip. It is priced per mask set, the unit at which a design becomes silicon, and one license covers every certified block in that mask, from the SoC down. The design goes only to a designer-named foundry, which must prove it received the committed package before it may consume a unit. The licensee never downloads the file. The floor is walked down as adoption grows, toward what a proof physically costs to produce and settle.

Fifty-eight machine-checked theorems bound what these licenses can charge and guarantee; they are on the research page.

Where it settles: three contracts on Arbitrum Stylus

Three contracts carry the economy, one per way of settling a proof on chain. All three compile to WebAssembly with the current Stylus toolchain and pass Arbitrum's validation check on the Sepolia test network; deployment waits on human review, and on Arbitrum reopening Stylus activation, which the network paused on October 2, 2026.

Cell differentiation, free through your first fabrication run

The same function comes in many cells. sky130 alone ships its strongest buffer, buf_16, in five standard-cell libraries, each built for a different job. Knowing which one a given net should use has lived inside foundries and EDA vendors. Q2 measures the candidates for the net you describe, on one open bench anyone can rerun, and publishes the comparison. The service is free of charge to a team through its first fabrication run, so choosing cells well is not the barrier to getting a first design made.

The result for buf_16. Typical corner, 250 fF load, every figure against hd, the high-density library that is the usual default:

−26%
delay of hs, the high-speed library, for 1.1% more energy per switch
78×

+28%
area of hs, ms and ls, which sit in taller rows than hd

What that means for a net. On a busy net where timing is tight, hs cuts a quarter of the delay for about one percent more energy per switch. On a net that mostly rests, hs draws at least twice hd's power once the net switches fewer than about 2,900 times per second at the typical corner (99,000 at the fast corner), and hd is the right cell. Where area is the constraint, hd again. hdll is slower than hd at this drive and halves leakage only at the fast corner. The power of a net is switches per second times energy per switch, plus leakage times 1.8 V. All three corners and all three loads are in results/differentiate.md.

After your first spin. A design you list earns from every manufacturing license. The composition contract credits your address directly and you withdraw it yourself; Q2 never holds it. Q2 is paid for the service, through a stated fee for first-spin assistance after the free differentiation, not through a share of what your design earns.

What the anchor records

A comparison is useful when anyone can check that it was not edited afterward. The run is committed as one hash for a Stylus contract on Arbitrum to record. The hash covers the bench code, the five netlists by their sha256, the PDK version, the operating points, and every measured number. All of it is public in the repository, so anyone can rerun the bench and recompute the same hash.

record(bytes32 artifact_hash, uint16 label, uint16 context)
// buf_16 in five sky130 libraries, 3 corners x 3 loads
record(0xb9968a68...aaf531, 1, 16)
//     hash of the bench bundle   1 = measurement   drive strength

label 1 marks a measurement record and carries no improvement claim. context 16 is the drive strength of the cells compared. The contract is written in Rust for Arbitrum Stylus and passes cargo stylus check. Status: not deployed. analysis/differentiate.py writes this record() call to results/differentiate.json; submitting it needs a funded key.
