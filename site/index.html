The Proof Economy

Prove a circuit once, in public, and let every system built on it inherit the proof.

A chip is a hierarchy of blocks, and before any of it is sent to a foundry each block must be shown correct: a mask set at a modern node costs one to two million dollars, and industry surveys put first-silicon success at 14 percent in 2024 and 5 percent in 2026. Today that verification is repeated. Every company that uses an adder or a floating-point unit re-verifies its own copy in private, and the work is thrown away when the project ends. A proof of a block does not depend on who uses the block. If the proof is public and content-addressed, the next team inherits it instead of repeating it, and a larger system is proved by reference to the proofs of its parts without ever opening them. This site is the public instance of that idea: how a proof is graded, what a certificate is worth, where it settles, and one cell taken through the whole loop.

Two words, then the ladder

A block is any unit of the hierarchy with an interface of its own: a cell of a few transistors, a component such as an adder or a decoder, an assembly such as a multiplier, a system such as an accelerator. A certificate is the public record of a block's proof: the content hash of its design, the content hash of its interface, the level at which it was proved, and the content hash of the proof of record. A certificate for a composed block also names the certificates of its children. Anyone can check that the proof exists and matches; nobody needs the design to do so.

How a proof is graded: the levels of proof

Every certificate names the level at which its block is proved. The ladder runs from the strongest bounded proof to the one that scales without bound. Exhaustive checking costs 2 to the power of the input width, so it stops at small blocks; composition is the top and it is closed: a composed block is a part for the next composition.

What a certificate is worth: two licenses

Every certificate carries two licenses. The protocol, meaning the contracts that settle them, takes nothing from either.

Reference: the commons

Cost recovery, never rent

The reference license is the right to name a certificate as a child of a larger proof. Foundry PDK primitives are free to reference forever. Every other certificate recovers only its listing gas, the on-chain fee its lister paid to list it, from the certificates one level up that use it directly; the recursion beneath is never charged. Collection stops at that gas, then the certificate is free. Whoever lists a better implementation of the same interface pays the remaining listing cost of the block it beats: beat or fork.

Manufacturing: the designer's product

$100 per mask set, SoC down

The manufacturing license is the right to put a design into a fabricated system on chip. It is priced per mask set, the unit at which a design becomes silicon, and one license covers every certified block in that mask, from the SoC down. The design goes only to a designer-named foundry, which must prove it received the committed package before it may consume a unit. The licensee never downloads the file. The floor is walked down as adoption grows, toward what a proof physically costs to produce and settle.

Fifty-eight machine-checked theorems bound what these licenses can charge and guarantee; they are on the research page.

Where it settles: three contracts on Arbitrum Stylus

Three contracts carry the economy, one per way of settling a proof on chain. All three compile to WebAssembly with the current Stylus toolchain and pass Arbitrum's validation check on the Sepolia test network; deployment waits on human review.

The economy in practice: beat or fork on one cell

The rule above says anyone may list a better implementation of an existing interface, provided the improvement is measured and the prior lister is made whole. Here is that rule run end to end on a single sky130 standard cell, the foundry's buf_16 driver. An agent proposes candidate cells, an independent oracle measures them against the foundry's own, and each verified win is committed for the chain to record. The three steps are the three pages of this site.

What the anchor records

The point of a win is not a screenshot, it is a durable, verifiable record. Each verified improvement is committed as a hash for a Stylus contract on Arbitrum to record. Anyone holding the netlist can recompute the hash and check it; no one else can read the design. This is the anchor contract from the table above, applied to the best win of the loop.

record(bytes32 artifact_hash, uint16 label, uint16 context)
// best in-band win: EDP +22% vs buf_16, same area
record(0xbbe5a7f1...f8f7c, 2200, 16)
//         hash of the design bundle   basis points   drive strength

label is basis points of EDP improvement, context is the part's drive strength. The hash is the artifact; the netlist never has to be published. The contract is written in Rust for Arbitrum Stylus and passes cargo stylus check. Status: not deployed. anchor.py prepares this record() call, it does not submit it (that needs a funded key)
